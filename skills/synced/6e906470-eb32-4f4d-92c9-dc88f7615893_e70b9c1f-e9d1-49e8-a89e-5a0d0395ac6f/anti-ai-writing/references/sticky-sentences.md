# Sticky Sentences - Extended Guide

Memorable writing uses specific techniques that make ideas lodge in the reader's mind. These aren't tricks - they're patterns that humans have used for thousands of years.

## Technique 1: Alliteration

Same starting sounds create rhythm and memorability.

- "Specificity is the secret"
- "The best jobs are neither decreed nor degreed"
- "Practice makes permanent" (not perfect - permanent is more memorable because it's unexpected)
- "Fail fast, fix forward"

When to use: Headlines, key takeaways, phrases you want people to repeat.

When to avoid: More than one alliterative phrase per section. It gets noticeable and gimmicky.

## Technique 2: Symmetry

Parallel structure creates balance. The reader expects the pattern and feels satisfied when it lands.

- "Read for awareness. Write for understanding."
- "It's not 10,000 hours. It's 10,000 iterations."
- "Easy to learn. Hard to master."
- "What got you here won't get you there."

When to use: Contrasting two ideas, summarising a key insight, section closers.

When to avoid: Don't force symmetry when the ideas aren't naturally parallel. It reads as artificial.

## Technique 3: Contrast

Opposing ideas placed side by side create tension and clarity.

- "To be everywhere is to be nowhere."
- "Be clear, not clever. Concise, not complex."
- "The problem isn't the technology. The problem is how we use it."
- "Simple to say. Difficult to do."

When to use: Challenging assumptions, making a counterintuitive point, thesis statements.

When to avoid: Don't use false contrasts. "It's not about the destination, it's about the journey" is a cliche, not a contrast.

## Technique 4: Rhyme

Similar ending sounds make phrases feel complete and memorable.

- "Tell a story or lose your glory"
- "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit"
- "Fail to plan, plan to fail"

When to use: Sparingly. One rhyming phrase per piece maximum. More than that and you sound like a greeting card.

When to avoid: Most of the time. Rhyme works best in spoken content or headlines. In body text it feels forced.

## Technique 5: Rhythm

Pleasing cadence that pulls the reader forward.

- "When you can't wait to share it, they can't help but read it"
- "Ship early. Ship often. Fix what breaks."
- "They came. They saw. They left confused." (Tricolon with a twist)

When to use: Opening sentences, closing sentences, key messages.

When to avoid: Don't write in iambic pentameter. Natural rhythm variation is the goal, not poetry.

## Combining Techniques

The strongest memorable phrases combine two or more techniques:

- "Anger prepares us to fight. Fear prepares us to flee." (Symmetry + Alliteration)
- "Be clear, not clever. Concise, not complex." (Contrast + Alliteration + Symmetry)
- "Read for awareness. Write for understanding." (Symmetry + Contrast)

## Application Rules

1. Identify the 2-3 most important statements in your piece
2. Try each technique on those statements
3. Pick the version that feels most natural
4. Don't force it - if none of the techniques improve the sentence, leave it as plain prose
5. Maximum 3 sticky sentences per 1000 words. More than that dilutes the effect.

## Common Mistakes

- Using sticky sentence techniques on every sentence (makes the whole piece feel over-produced)
- Forcing alliteration with awkward word choices ("paramount paradigm proliferation")
- Using rhyme in formal business writing (save it for presentations)
- Creating symmetry that sacrifices clarity for balance
- Combining more than two techniques in one phrase (it gets too clever)

The goal is 2-3 memorable moments in an otherwise natural, conversational piece. The sticky sentences should feel effortless, not engineered.
